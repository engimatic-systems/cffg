Two junctions with the same name

Imagine starting on a square marked Amber and being asked to find a square marked Blue. You can move north, south, east or west. Two different crossroads along the way both have the sign junction.

Our traveler is a computer program. It gets two practice walks around the same 24 squares. At each step it can read the sign underfoot and tell whether its move succeeded. It starts at a known spot and can count how many squares it has moved in each direction. That tells it exactly where it is. Later, it gets six trips to make, with at most 24 moves per trip.

What should the traveler remember?

Think of a model as a notebook of directions. During practice the traveler writes down where each turn takes it. We gave it three ways to organize those notes.

  1. One entry per sign. Both crossroads go on the page marked “junction.” In the first layout, the traveler went north from a junction eight times. Four times it arrived at Brass. Four times it arrived at Slate. Later, whenever it reads “junction,” it consults that same page, wherever it is standing.
  2. Separate junction entries. The traveler uses its position to keep one junction page for the western crossroads and another for the eastern one. Now the notes say that north from the western junction leads to Brass, and north from the eastern junction leads to Slate. All the other signs are filed just as before.
  3. One entry per square. This traveler records the position of every square it visits and which steps connect them. It can then find the shortest route along the connections it has walked, even if it has never walked that whole route in one go.

One trip explains the difference

From Amber, all three travelers go south through Brass and arrive at the western junction on move two. At the eastern junction, two steps north would take it through Slate to Blue. The traveler with a single “junction” page tries north and finds itself back at Brass. It keeps doubling back and runs out of moves. The traveler with two junction pages heads east, crosses to the other junction, then turns north through Slate. It reaches Blue in ten moves.

What did the program learn?

We supplied the movement rules and the idea of giving the junctions separate pages. The traveler learned what to write on those pages from its walks. Our software, CFFG, compared the first two notebooks by asking each one to predict the next sign on two fresh walks after practice. The more often a notebook put a high chance on the sign that actually appeared, the better it scored. A separate route planner used each notebook to choose turns. We counted how often those choices got the traveler to its destination.

How the study works

  1. Learn from two practice walks

    The traveler starts at Home and follows a rule we wrote for exploring. It tries every direction at each square and records the outcome, including attempts to walk through a wall.

  2. Choose a notebook

    On two more walks we supply the turns. Both notebooks get exactly the same questions, such as “From this junction, what sign will I see if I go north?” We choose the one with the better prediction score. A tie goes to the simpler notebook.

  3. Try the notebooks out

    We check predictions on four further walks. Then we give each traveler six destinations and let it choose its own turns. Each traveler keeps the notes it made during practice.

Where the research question came from

One research group taught a computer to recognize short sequences that repeat. That suggests trying a traveler that remembers its last few turns. That history might help it distinguish two junctions even when their exits look alike. Read the RGM paper · See its Pong example

Another group built a robot that uses what it senses to add places to a map. As it moves, it learns which places it can travel between. That inspired our question about when it helps to record two places separately. Read how AIMAPP builds its map · See the robot’s route test
